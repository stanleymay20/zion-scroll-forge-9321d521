## Ignition

Here is the opening question of this course, and it will come back to haunt you until you can answer it in your own words: **How do you know what you know?**

Hook: most of what you believe about the world, you believe for one of three reasons. Someone you trust told you. You read it or saw it and it stuck. Or you tested it yourself and watched it hold up. Those three sources — trust, exposure, testing — are not the same, but we almost always treat them the same, as if "I heard it" and "I proved it" were two ways of saying the thing. They are not. The first module of this course is about that difference, and about one specific kind of knowing: *scholarship*. Before you can write a scholarly paper, you have to be able to see one when it is in front of you — and see the difference between it and the many things that merely look like one.

## What this module does

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. Define scholarship in your own words, using at least three of its distinguishing marks.
2. Tell the difference between a scholarly claim and an assertion — even a confident, loud, or popular one.
3. Name the four conditions a piece of writing must meet for us to call it scholarly in this course.
4. Recognize three common things that are often confused with scholarship, and say why the confusion is costly.

You should plan on this module taking about three focused hours — not three hours of scrolling, three hours of sitting, reading, and taking notes with your phone in another room. Part 2 of this course will teach you a note-taking system that survives revision; for Module 1, just read.

## Part 1: The scholarly disposition

Scholarship is not primarily a kind of writing. It is primarily a *disposition* — a stable, trainable way of standing toward what you do not yet know. The writing comes second, after the disposition has produced something worth writing down.

The disposition has three parts.

**Patience with evidence.** The scholar is willing to wait before concluding. "I don't know yet" is not a weakness in scholarship; it is the default position. The beginner treats every question as an emergency that must be answered right now. The scholar treats most questions as invitations that will yield more if held.

**Willingness to be wrong in public.** A scholar's claims are *falsifiable* and *corrigible* — someone else can show them to be wrong, and the scholar, when shown, revises. This is harder than it sounds. Most of us will die of embarrassment before we will die of being wrong. Scholarship asks you to invert that ratio.

**Transparency about how you got here.** The scholar shows their work. Where did this evidence come from? What was its source? Who said it? Was it measured, counted, witnessed, or inferred? What would it take to disprove it? If you cannot answer those questions for a claim you are making, the claim is not scholarly — however true it may happen to be.

These three — patience, corrigibility, transparency — are what distinguish scholarship from assertion. An assertion is a sentence someone says. Scholarship is a sentence someone says together with the honest backing that lets you check whether to believe them.

## Part 2: The marks of scholarship

For a piece of writing to count as scholarly in this course, it must satisfy four conditions. Think of these as the minimum invoice. Fewer, and you have something else — journalism, opinion, exegesis, testimony, memoir — all of which may be valuable, but none of which is scholarship.

1. **Evidence beyond the author's say-so.** The claim rests on something other than "I think so" or "because I said so." That something — a document, a measurement, a corpus, a precedent, a text, an experiment — is identifiable and (in principle) reachable by the reader.

2. **A visible method.** You can see, as a reader, how the author moved from evidence to conclusion. Not every step, but enough that a serious reader could follow the reasoning and, if motivated, redo it or challenge it.

3. **Named sources.** Where claims come from other people's work, the reader can tell *whose* work, *where* it was published, and (often) *when*. Modern conventions call this "citing sources," but the deeper reason is accountability. If you borrow a claim, the lender has a right to be named.

4. **A standing invitation to be corrected.** The piece is written in a way that makes revision possible — qualified claims, acknowledged limitations, explicit scope. Scholarship does not pretend to be the last word. It participates in a conversation that is still open.

A piece of writing that satisfies all four is scholarship. A piece that satisfies three is close — usually it is scholarship with a flaw, which is to say, scholarship. A piece that satisfies two or fewer is something else, and should be labeled as such before you build on it.

## Demonstration: a worked example

Consider two short pieces of writing about the same subject — call it the claim "regular prayer improves mental health."

**Piece A (viral post, 2024).** "People who pray every day are less depressed. The research proves it. Share this if you agree." Attached: a photograph of a sunset, 47,000 likes.

**Piece B (journal article, 2019).** A 22-page study published in a peer-reviewed clinical psychology journal. The authors recruited 412 participants from four religious traditions and one secular control group. They measured self-reported anxiety and depression over 18 months using the standardized PHQ-9 and GAD-7 instruments. They found a small, statistically significant correlation between daily prayer practice and reduced depression scores among the religious participants, and no significant effect among the secular control. They note four limitations: self-report bias, selection effects (people who pray daily may differ from those who don't in ways other than prayer), the correlational design (they cannot show prayer causes the reduction, only that they move together), and the narrow religious range. They invite replication in other traditions.

Both pieces make versions of a similar claim. Only one of them is scholarship. Why?

Piece A asserts a conclusion ("the research proves it") without evidence the reader can reach, without a method the reader can see, without named sources the reader can check, and without any acknowledgement that it might be wrong. It is persuasion, not scholarship. That does not make it worthless — persuasion has its place — but it means you cannot build further argument on Piece A without first going and finding Piece B or something like it.

Piece B puts all four marks on the table. You can find the journal. You can read the method. You can check who wrote it and where they work. You can disagree with the conclusions, and if you do, the authors have already given you some of the tools to argue back (the four limitations are gifts to future scholars).

The costly mistake is to read Piece A and treat it as if it had the authority of Piece B. That mistake gets made every day, in every discipline, by people who do not want to do the work of distinguishing. Part of your job in this course is to refuse to make that mistake.

## Part 3: What scholarship is not

Three things get confused with scholarship often enough that it is worth naming them.

**Expert opinion is not scholarship.** An expert is someone with deep experience in a domain. When they speak within their domain, they are usually worth listening to. But *an expert asserting a conclusion* is not the same as *the expert's scholarly work*. A climate scientist saying "we have forty years" in a podcast is doing one thing; the paper they wrote with four co-authors over three years is doing another. Both can be true, but only the second one is scholarship — because only the second one shows its work and invites correction.

**Consensus is not scholarship.** "Everyone in the field agrees" is a useful signal — it tells you where the burden of proof sits — but it is not itself the argument. Scholars track the consensus not to settle questions but to know where the live questions have moved. Treating consensus as the final word short-circuits the habit this course exists to build.

**Authority is not scholarship.** "Because St. Augustine said so" or "because the textbook said so" is the beginning of an argument, not the end. Scholarship respects authorities, but it does not substitute them for evidence and reasoning. If St. Augustine said so, that is one data point; what reasons did he give, what evidence did he have, and does it still hold up under the kinds of questions a careful reader asks today?

The Christian scholarly tradition has always been emphatic on this point, which can surprise people. Augustine himself wrote that he would rather doubt a thing and come to truth than believe it on insufficient grounds and be comforted by it. Aquinas structured his *Summa* around objections precisely because he wanted every position to earn its ground against the strongest counter-arguments available. The tradition takes scholarship seriously *because* it takes truth seriously; it will not accept a cheap comfort in place of a hard-won clarity.

## Scripture

The biblical witness consistently commends careful inquiry rather than credulous assent. Proverbs 18:17 puts it directly: *"The one who states his case first seems right, until the other comes and examines him."* The whole point of scholarship is that the examination comes next. We do not settle questions by who speaks loudest or first. We settle them by letting the examination happen.

The Berean believers in Acts 17 are commended for exactly this habit — Luke calls them "more noble" than the Thessalonians because they "received the message with great eagerness and examined the Scriptures every day to see if what Paul said was true." Not because they rejected Paul. Because they checked him. That is the disposition this course trains in you, carried over into secular inquiry: receive eagerly, examine carefully, take nothing as settled before you have done the work of seeing for yourself.

This does not mean that scholarship and faith are the same thing. They are not. Faith makes claims about what is ultimately true in ways that outrun what any argument can establish; scholarship makes claims about what we have reason to believe given the evidence currently in view. Both are honest practices when they know what they are. The trouble starts when we blur them — when we dress up a conviction in the costume of an argument, or when we treat a well-argued case as if it had the finality of revelation. This course asks you to keep them distinct in your writing. Argue what you can argue. Confess what you confess. Do not confuse the two.

## Activation — your turn

Try this. Open a news site, a social-media feed, or a video platform. Find the first article, post, or video that makes a strong claim about the world — a claim with causal weight, like "X causes Y" or "A is the reason for B." Spend ten minutes on it.

Then answer these questions in writing:

1. What is the claim, in one sentence? (Not the headline — the claim.)
2. What evidence does the piece cite, if any?
3. Who is the named source for that evidence?
4. What would it take to show the claim wrong? Does the piece tell you?

Most pieces will fail three of the four questions. That is not a failure of the pieces — most of them are not trying to be scholarship — but it is worth seeing how far outside the scholarly envelope the normal information diet sits. You cannot write scholarship if you cannot see the difference.

## Reflect

Set a timer for five minutes. Write, by hand or in a plain text file (not a chat window), a response to this prompt:

> Think of a belief you hold strongly about the world — not a religious belief, a factual one. Something about how people behave, how economies work, how a historical event unfolded. Why do you believe it? Walk back through the chain of how it reached you. At the bottom of that chain, is there scholarship? Or is there a voice you trust?

There is no wrong answer. The point is to notice. Most of what you know, you know on trust. That is normal — no one has time to re-derive everything from first principles. The scholar's job is not to distrust trust; it is to know when the question in front of you is important enough that trust is not enough, and evidence has to be gathered.
